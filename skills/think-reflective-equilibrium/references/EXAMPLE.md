# Reflective Equilibrium - Coherence Set + Revision Ledger - Worked Example

A completed run of `think-reflective-equilibrium`, on the shared Northwind scenario. This is the quality bar a generated run should meet: the caveat leads, all three tiers are present, the revision is bidirectional, and the ledger records which commitment gave way and why.

> Northwind is a B2B SaaS whose stated layoff policy is "last in, first out" (LIFO), but a proposed cut would remove the entire night-shift support pod, who are recent hires yet the only staff covering a major enterprise account's contracted off-hours SLA.

> **Evidence caveat (read first):** Reflective equilibrium is tier C: philosophically central since Rawls (1971) but empirically untested as a procedure. "Equilibrium" has no external stopping test (it cannot be told apart from "I stopped looking"), the inputs can be a reshuffling of prejudices (Brandt 1979), and the method can license rationalizing a preferred outcome. Even idealized, convergence is rare (Freivogel 2023, ~27 percent). What follows is worth reading only because the revision ledger shows which commitment gave way and why; the bare claim "we reached coherence" would not be.

---

## The question

- Should Northwind apply its LIFO layoff policy to the night-shift pod, or is the strong judgment that doing so is unfair load-bearing enough to revise the policy?

## Tier a - Considered judgments (particular cases)

- Cutting the entire night-shift pod to honor LIFO is unfair to people doing irreplaceable, contracted work `[held strongly]`
- A layoff policy that managers can quietly override case by case is itself unfair (it invites favoritism) `[held strongly]`
- Keeping a clearly underperforming recent hire purely because of their shift would be unjustifiable `[held weakly]`

## Tier b - General principles

- Apply layoff selection by a single objective rule (LIFO) so no one is singled out `[held weakly]`
- Treat equal contributors equally; do not penalize people for the shift they were assigned `[held strongly]`
- Do not break a contracted obligation (the enterprise SLA) to satisfy an internal procedure `[held strongly]`

## Tier c - Background theories (the wide variant: what the principles are pressure-tested against)

- Procedural fairness theory: rules constrain managerial discretion precisely so that outcomes are not bent toward favorites; this is why the LIFO principle had appeal.
- A theory of desert: tenure is a proxy for contribution, not contribution itself; when the proxy and the thing it stands for come apart, the proxy loses its claim.
- Obligation theory: a freely-made contractual commitment (the SLA) generates a duty that an internal convenience rule does not outrank.

## Conflicts found

- The case judgment "cutting the night-shift pod is unfair" collides with the principle "apply layoffs by a single objective rule (LIFO)."
- The case judgment "ad hoc manager overrides are themselves unfair" collides with simply abandoning LIFO for discretion.

## Revision ledger (which commitment gave way, and why)

| Conflict | Commitment that gave way | Direction | Why it was the weaker member | Alternative considered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Cutting the pod is unfair" vs. "apply layoffs strictly by LIFO" | The LIFO principle | case revises principle | LIFO was held weakly and rests on tenure as a proxy for contribution; the background desert theory shows the proxy fails here, where the recent hires are the irreplaceable contributors | Overruling the case judgment and applying LIFO anyway, rejected because the unfairness judgment was held strongly and the SLA obligation reinforced it |
| "Ad hoc overrides are unfair" vs. "just give managers discretion" | The case judgment (only its broad form) | principle overrules case | The worry is real but does not require pure rule-following; a *stated, written* criterion (protect contracted-SLA roles) is still an objective rule, so the anti-favoritism principle survives without LIFO | Letting managers decide pod by pod, rejected because it re-imports the favoritism the case judgment rightly fears |

## Residual disagreements (stop honestly)

- Whether "protect contracted-SLA roles" generalizes to a fair company-wide successor to LIFO, or is a one-off carve-out, is not resolved here. I reconciled the night-shift conflict; I did not produce a vetted general layoff policy. Coherence is local to this case, not global.

## What this run does and does not warrant

- The coherent position reached: revise LIFO to a written rule that exempts roles holding a live contractual obligation, rather than override case by case. This is warranted only as far as the starting judgments are sound; the run does not prove the night-shift judgment is correct, only that the set now hangs together once the weaker, proxy-based principle gives way. The honest output is the ledger above, not the word "coherence."

---

*Note: the value of this run is not the conclusion, it is the visible accounting of what was revised. Without the ledger, a motivated reader could have demoted the SLA principle instead and reported an equally "coherent" result that favored the cut, which is exactly the rationalization failure mode (Brandt 1979) the caveat warns about. If you next need an impartial successor rule for layoffs generally, `think-veil-of-ignorance-reasoning` is the stronger move.*

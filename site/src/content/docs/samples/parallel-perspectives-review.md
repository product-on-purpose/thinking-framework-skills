## Situation

Priya is an analyst at a city transit agency. The board wants a recommendation on a vendor pilot that would put facial-recognition cameras in three busy transit hubs to flag people on a fare-evasion watchlist. Privacy advocates are loud and the transit-police union is enthusiastic, and Priya is worried that one of those voices will end up writing the whole memo before the quieter considerations get a hearing.

## Prompt

```
/think-parallel-perspectives-review "Should our agency run the vendor's facial-recognition pilot in three transit hubs to flag watchlisted fare-evaders? Privacy groups are against it, the transit-police union is for it, and I need a balanced read for the board before either side drowns out the rest."
```

## The artifact

## Under review

- Whether to run a 90-day facial-recognition pilot in three transit hubs to flag watchlisted fare-evaders for staff intervention.

## Lenses

| Lens | What it surfaces |
|---|---|
| Facts | Fare evasion is up 14% year over year at the three hubs; the watchlist holds about 600 names sourced from prior citations. The vendor cites 98% match accuracy on its own benchmark, not on our demographics or our camera angles. Missing: false-positive rate by skin tone and lighting, the legal basis for retention, and what staff actually do when a match fires. |
| Upside | If matches are accurate and interventions are lawful, the agency recovers fare revenue, deters repeat evasion at the worst hubs, and gets a defensible data trail for the board's revenue mandate. |
| Risks | A false positive stops or accuses an innocent rider, and documented demographic accuracy gaps make wrongful stops likely to fall unevenly. Watchlist scope can creep beyond fare evasion; a breach exposes biometric data that riders cannot reissue; and public trust, once lost on surveillance, is slow to rebuild. |
| Intuition | This reads as a revenue problem being answered with a surveillance tool because the vendor was already in the room. The gut sense is that the board wants to be seen acting on evasion, and "facial recognition" is the visible move, not the proportionate one. |
| Alternatives | Targeted staffing and gate redesign at the three hubs; fare-capping or discounted passes that remove the incentive to evade; a non-biometric tap-validation audit; a narrow human-review pilot with no automated stop. Several are cheaper and far easier to unwind. |
| Process | This is close to a one-way door on public trust and biometric data. The big-picture move is to get the missing demographic-accuracy and legal-basis facts, and to weigh the lighter alternatives, before committing the agency's name to a biometric pilot. |

## Synthesis

The upside is conditional on an accuracy claim the facts cannot yet confirm on our riders, while the risks land hardest on exactly the people a false match would wrongly stop, and the intuition lens flags that a revenue goal is being met with a surveillance tool by default. The central tension is proportionality versus visible action: the board wants a decisive move on evasion, but a biometric pilot is hard to unwind and unevenly risky. Resolve it by commissioning an independent demographic-accuracy and legal-basis assessment and costing the two lightest alternatives (gate redesign, fare-capping) before any biometric pilot is approved, rather than treating the vendor pilot as the default answer.

---

*Note: this review transfers evidence from human decision contexts and is not AI-validated; the value here is the intuition and alternatives lenses, which a privacy-versus-revenue shouting match would have skipped, and which reframed the choice from "deploy or don't" to "prove proportionality first."*

## Why this framework fits

The job here is to stop the two loudest voices, privacy advocates and the police union, from collapsing a multi-dimensional decision into a single axis. Running the lenses separately forces the quiet intuition and alternatives passes to surface a cheaper, more reversible path that an unaided privacy-versus-revenue debate would never have reached.

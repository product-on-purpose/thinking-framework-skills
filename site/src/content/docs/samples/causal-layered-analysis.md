> A compact worked example of [Causal Layered Analysis](../../frameworks/think-causal-layered-analysis/) on a realistic situation. For full end-to-end narratives see the [Showcase](../../showcase/); for the evidence behind the method see its [framework page](../../frameworks/think-causal-layered-analysis/).

## Situation

Priya analyzes housing policy for a mid-sized city. For a decade the council has fought the same fight every budget cycle: build more emergency shelter beds versus enforce camping bans and "clean up" encampments. Both camps cite the same point-in-time count, both quote the same cost-per-bed figures, and nothing about how the city sees the problem ever shifts - the debate just re-runs at higher volume while the unsheltered count climbs. Priya is not trying to decide this year's budget line; she wants to break the framing that keeps producing the same stuck argument, so she runs the analysis by hand on paper before the next council retreat.

## Prompt

```text
/think-causal-layered-analysis "Our city has argued 'more shelter beds vs. enforce the camping ban' for ten years. Every budget cycle re-runs the same fight - same point-in-time count, same cost-per-bed numbers - and the framing never moves while the unsheltered count keeps rising. This isn't a numbers disagreement, it's a clash about what homelessness even is. I want to read the issue at the litany / system / worldview / myth layers, surface whose framing is privileged and what it hides, then deliberately change the deep metaphor and rebuild a preferred-future stack from it. Stakeholders: council fiscal-conservative bloc, the housing-first advocates, the downtown business association, the unhoused residents themselves, and the shelter nonprofits. Reframe it - do not decide the budget."
```

## The artifact

> The deep readings (worldview and myth) are interpretive and contestable by design. Do NOT present "the real myth" as a discovered fact. Hold competing readings, name whose worldview is privileged, and stop at the reframe - this is not a forecast, a ranking, or a decision.

### Issue and fit

- **Issue:** For ten years the city has argued "more emergency shelter beds vs. enforce the camping ban," and every budget cycle re-runs the identical fight while the unsheltered count rises - because the two camps are not actually disagreeing about the numbers.
- **Why CLA fits:** The issue is contested, value-laden, and long-horizon, and the *framing* is what is stuck - both sides have exhausted the litany (the count, the cost-per-bed figures) and the system (shelter capacity, enforcement, zoning), and the live disagreement is a worldview clash about what homelessness *is* and whose city this is. It is not a simple, technical, or single-cause problem, and the goal here is to open a different way of seeing it, not to optimize this cycle's shelter line.
- **Whose framing is in play:** The fiscal-conservative council bloc and the downtown business association ("disorder to be managed and moved") versus the housing-first advocates and shelter nonprofits ("a housing shortage to be ended"), with the unhoused residents themselves rarely framing the issue at all - they are framed *about*. CLA surfaces that the official "beds vs. ban" litany belongs mostly to the management camp, which sets the agenda's terms.

### The four-layer matrix

| Layer | Current - the "used future" | Reconstructed - the preferred future |
|---|---|---|
| **Litany** (the official, visible, unquestioned account: headlines, accepted problem statement, "everyone knows") | "The unsheltered count is up again, encampments are a public-safety and sanitation crisis downtown, shelters are full, and the camping ban isn't being enforced. Everyone knows we either need more beds or we need to clear the camps - the question is just which, and at what cost." The visible, repeated account, framed entirely as a shelter-capacity-vs-enforcement gap. | "How many of our neighbors moved from the street into stable housing this year, and how fast?" The surface conversation shifts from bed counts and sweep counts to housing placements, time-to-housing, and returns-to-homelessness - the city reports on people housed, not encampments cleared. |
| **System** (structural and short-term causes: policies, economics, institutions, incentives, processes) | Shelter contracts paid per bed-night, an enforcement budget for sweeps and citations, zoning that blocks dense and supportive housing, a homelessness response housed in public-works and police rather than housing, and an annual point-in-time count that drives funding. The structures quietly assume the response is *temporary crisis management*, so they fund beds and sweeps, not homes. | Permanent supportive and deeply affordable housing as the funded backbone, contracts and incentives paid on *housing outcomes and retention* rather than bed-nights or citations, zoning reform to allow the supply, and the response relocated from public-works/police into a housing-and-health agency. The "manage the crisis" assumption is replaced by "end the condition." |
| **Worldview / discourse** (deeper ideological and paradigmatic assumptions; *whose* worldview is privileged; competing discourses) | Privileged worldview: **homelessness as disorder** - "unhoused people are a public-order and public-health problem to be contained, moved, and made less visible; the city's duty is to its taxpayers and its downtown." The crowded-out worldview: **homelessness as a housing-market failure and a rights question** - "people are unhoused because housing is scarce and unaffordable, and they are residents owed a place, not a nuisance to relocate." The fight feels unwinnable because each side is defending a moral identity of the city, not a tactic. (A third reading is also present: the unhoused residents' own worldview, **survival under conditions others designed**, which the official litany rarely admits at all.) | A reconciling worldview: **the city succeeds when its residents are housed**, so an unhoused neighbor is evidence of a system that has not yet done its job, not a disorder to be moved. "Order downtown" and "people housed" stop being opposed, because durable order follows from people actually being housed rather than displaced to the next block. |
| **Myth / metaphor** (the unconscious, emotive, civilizational story and guiding metaphor - a metaphor, not a proposition) | The used-future metaphor: **"the city as a clean house, and the unhoused as a mess to sweep."** Homelessness is dirt; the response is sanitation; success is a tidy, swept street. (Offered as one reading; an alternative deep metaphor in play is "the city as a fortress," the business association's image of a downtown defended against an outside threat.) | The deliberately changed metaphor: **"the city as a household where everyone has a bed."** A household does not sweep its own members out the door; if a family member has nowhere to sleep, the household has failed *itself*, and the work is to make room, not to make the person disappear. |

### The anchor: the changed deep metaphor

- **Old metaphor (used future):** "the city as a house to be cleaned, the unhoused as a mess to sweep" - homelessness is dirt, the response is sanitation, and the whole fight is how aggressively to sweep versus how many beds to provide as a holding pen.
- **New metaphor (preferred future):** "the city as a household where everyone has a bed" - an unhoused neighbor is a household member without a bed, and the city's job is to make room.
- **Why this change:** the cleaning metaphor forces a sweep-vs-store-them either/or and makes the *person* the thing to be removed; the household metaphor dissolves that, because a household's natural response to someone with nowhere to sleep is to find them a bed, not to push them out - which turns "beds vs. ban" into a question of how fast the city can actually house people rather than a war over how visible they are.

### Reading order and integrity check

- Read **down** the current column first (litany to myth), then **change the metaphor** ("sweep the mess" to "a household where everyone has a bed"), then reconstruct **up** the preferred column (new myth to new litany). Each preferred-future layer above follows from the household metaphor, not from the old cleaning stack.
- **Contestability note:** "the city as a house to be cleaned" is one reading of the deep story, not a discovered fact; the business association's lived metaphor is closer to "the fortress" (a downtown defended against an outside threat), and the unhoused residents' own metaphor is different again. Naming these competing readings, rather than declaring one "the real myth," is the point - the reconstruction works by offering a metaphor ("the household") that the management camp can recognize too, since a household also wants order, not by proving one side's metaphor true.
- **Scope note:** this matrix reframes the stuck issue; it does not decide this cycle's budget, forecast the unsheltered count, or rank shelter-vs-housing spending. Once the framing has shifted from "how visible do we let them be" to "how fast can we house people," the actual allocation decision is handed to a decision tool, and the longer-horizon uncertainties (funding, housing supply, state policy) to a scenario method.

### Evidence caveat

> This four-layer matrix is a reframing aid. The method's evidence is conceptually plausible but untested (transferred from human futures and facilitation practice), with no measured outcome effect; the reconstructed preferred future is an interpretive reframe, not a forecast.

## Why this framework fits

The council was re-running the litany (the count) and the system (beds vs. enforcement) for a decade while the actual disagreement - a worldview clash about whether the unhoused are a mess to sweep or neighbors without a bed - sat unexamined underneath, which is exactly the framing-stuck condition CLA exists to break. The artifact gives Priya what the looping budget argument never could: a named privileged worldview and the readings it crowds out, plus a deliberately changed deep metaphor that rebuilds a preferred-future stack the old "beds vs. ban" frame made literally unsayable.
